The Original Code is Genesis3D

That line sits at the top of nearly every file in the engine, followed by a date: released March 25, 1999. Version 1.1 shipped that November and nothing came after it. It runs natively on Linux now, and this is how that happened.

It started because I wanted to tweak an old game

Not a project. Not a plan. I was playing something old and I wanted to change it, the way you do when a game is nearly right and you can see the one thing you would move. That question turns into a different question fast: what is this actually built on, and can I get at it.

Genesis3D is what I found, and what I found was a complete engine with its source published, sitting exactly where it was left in 1999. Not decompiled. Not reconstructed. The real thing, with the people who wrote it still visible in the comments. And completely unable to run on the machine in front of me.

So the tweak became the port. I did it with Gemini and Codex Sol, which is worth saying plainly because a job this size used to be the reason nobody attempted it. Three of us, one of whom is a person.

1999 is still in there, and it talks

Published engine source was rare. Most studios treated the renderer as the crown jewels. Eclipse Entertainment shipped theirs in full, so what survives is not a summary of how a 1999 engine worked. It is the actual working notes of the people building one, mid thought.

In Actor/body.c, someone is trying to work out lighting behaviour across the hardware of the day and gives up mid sentence:

point light and directional light don't seem much different, but in powervr they are... hhmmm...

PowerVR. That is a comment you can date to within about a year. In World/Trace.c, in the middle of the BSP traversal, someone knows exactly what the better version looks like and writes down why they are not doing it: the stack based approach could bail out early, but it's way too ugly. Two lines up: bsp ordering makes the magic happen. And in the OpenGL driver, at shutdown, a comment explains a glFinish by saying they are closing up shop.

If you wrote C in that decade, you have written all three of those comments. They are still compiling.

The logo they made impossible to remove

Open Engine/Logo and the folder is its own time capsule: CORONA.c, streak.c, electric.c, A_CORONA.c, A_STREAK.c. Lens flares and electric arcs, compiled into the engine, for the animated splash that played before your game did.

Next to them sits poweredby.c. Its entire contents are one array: unsigned char Poweredby_bmp[], a 256 by 256 bitmap written out as 65,656 comma separated integers in a C source file. Beside it, WebUrl.c does the same thing again for the words www.genesis3d.com.

They were not being careless. The licence requires that logo to play first, before your own, and to appear on your marketing. Embedding it as source meant it shipped with every build and could not be quietly left out of an install. Twenty seven years later, that decision is why the badge at the bottom of this page is not a redrawing of the Genesis3D logo. It is the original bytes, lifted straight back out of the array.

Four renderers, and one file that ruined it

Here is the part that decided whether any of this was possible.

Engine/Drivers holds four separate rendering backends: Direct3D, Direct3D 7, a wireframe debug driver, and OpenGL. In 1999, on Windows, the commercially sensible move was to pick Direct3D and commit everything to it. Eclipse instead defined a driver interface, put every renderer behind it, and shipped an OpenGL implementation that almost nobody was asking for.

The same instinct runs through the rest of it. The world is a BSP tree with precomputed lightmaps. Characters are actors with skeletal animation, held apart from world geometry. Collision is a swept hull query against the BSP rather than anything bolted to how a frame gets drawn. Simulation is kept separate from presentation. Every one of those is a seam, and seams are what let a stranger work on one part without detonating the rest. That is what publishing your source actually demands of the architecture.

And then, inside the OpenGL driver, next to the rendering code, sits a file called Win32.c. Every driver has one.

The engine could change graphics APIs freely. But every API was reached through Windows: the window, the message loop, the input, the timer, the surface it drew into. Genesis3D was portable in the layer everyone worries about and welded shut in the layer everyone forgets. That is the real reason twenty five years of good intentions never produced a Linux build. The job was never a graphics port. It was replacing the window, input and timing layers underneath a physics system nobody wanted to be the one to break.

The handover you can count

Eclipse Entertainment held the copyright from 1996 to 1999 and shipped 1.0 on 25 March 1999. WildTangent, Inc. bought the assets on 25 October and shipped 1.1 three weeks later, on 15 November. The technology went into Web Driver, their browser game platform, and Genesis3D stopped being a product with its own name on it.

You can watch that change hands in the headers. Count them in the 1.1 tree: 201 files read Copyright (C) 1999 WildTangent, Inc. Exactly two still say Eclipse Entertainment, Tclip.c and tclip.h. Nobody rewrites a 3D engine in the seven months between 1.0 and 1.1. The lines are Eclipse's work with a new name stamped over the top, and two files somebody missed.

Nothing was ever withdrawn. The source stayed where it was, the licence stayed what it had been, and the licence still names Eclipse as the party its terms are owed to. The engine was abandoned in place, which is the only reason any of this was still possible. It was not entirely alone either: the Reality Factory community picked it up after WildTangent walked away and kept it compiling for years.

What runs today

The renderer was not rewritten. Eclipse's OpenGL path was the road already built. What got replaced is everything that road ran through.

  • SDL2 owns the window and input. The Win32 message loop and its per driver platform glue are gone.
  • Swept BSP collision, proven again. Gravity, jumping, stairs, slopes, wall sliding and ceiling rejection, verified after the layers beneath them were swapped out.
  • Simulation pinned to 60 Hz. Actor motion and light styles advance on a fixed clock, whether the frame is presented at 60 or 120 FPS.
  • Lightmaps updated in place. Only dirty, visible tiles are re-uploaded. Geometry is never rebuilt.
  • Clean shutdown. SIGINT and SIGTERM release graphics, world and file resources, checked under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer.

A 3D engine is one of the few kinds of software that genuinely rots. It sits on graphics APIs, operating systems and hardware assumptions that all move without asking. When they move far enough the code stops being software and becomes a document: readable, studied, not runnable. Genesis3D sat in that state for about twenty five years. The seam Eclipse cut in 1998 held long enough to be used.

Come break it

This is the part I actually care about. The licence Eclipse wrote was designed to pull people in, and the whole point of getting the engine running again is that other people can now get at it.

Test it. Play with it. Change it. Run it on hardware I do not have. Tell me what breaks on NVIDIA, on Intel, on Wayland, on a distribution I have never installed. Build something stupid with it and show me. Open an issue that just says it crashed, because that is still more than I had yesterday.

If you want something concrete, the community roadmap lays out six areas with real work in each: reproducible builds and CI, renderer completeness, platform coverage, audio and gamepad support, the asset and authoring pipeline, and parser fuzzing. It also says plainly what is out of scope, because Genesis3D is an engine and weapons, levels and game balance belong to whatever gets built on it.

There is no committee and no gate. If you were writing C in 1999, this codebase will feel like coming back to a room you used to work in. If you were not, it is one of the few complete engines from that era you can read end to end without a decompiler.
